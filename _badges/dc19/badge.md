The DEF CON 19 badge (2011, Rio Hotel & Casino, Las Vegas) broke from the electronic-badge trend of the previous few years: designer Ryan "1o57" Clarke built it instead as a non-electronic puzzle object. Each badge was waterjet-cut from 0.040-inch commercially pure titanium sheets stacked four thick, tumbled to deburr the edges, and then oxidized in an industrial kiln at around 1000°F to give it its antiqued finish — all fabricated in the United States.

Rather than blinking LEDs, the badge carried clues, hints, and the opening moves of a weekend-long challenge, functioning as both admission credential and puzzle piece. DEF CON deliberately did not disclose how many design variants existed beyond the standard role set (Goon, Press, Vendor, Contest, Speaker, Human, Uber), encouraging attendees to compare badges and hunt for differences as part of the game.

No pricing, production quantity, or design files are published for this badge — it was a conference credential rather than a standalone product, and being a physical waterjet/kiln fabrication rather than a PCB, there is nothing like Gerbers or a BOM to share.

## History

This is one entry in the run of non-electronic, puzzle-focused DEF CON badges Ryan Clarke designed through the early-to-mid 2010s before DEF CON badges returned to electronics (e.g. DC20 with Parallax, DC24's robot skull).

## Notes merged from the duplicate entry "DEF CON 19 Official Badge"

The DEF CON 19 (2011) badge marked a deliberate break from the electronics-heavy designs of prior years. Designer Ryan "1o57" Clarke moved the badge to a non-electronic, puzzle-based reality game: each badge is a stack of four 0.040" commercially pure titanium sheets, waterjet-cut and then kiln-oxidized at 1000°F to give the metal an aged, worn look supporting the game's ambience.

Badges were issued across the standard DEF CON attendee categories — Goon, Press, Vendor, Contest, Speaker, Human, and Uber — with each category carrying its own design, and DEF CON's own materials note that the total number of distinct designs actually made was never published. The badge anchored a live reality game that ran through the conference, including a professional actor performing in character and puzzles/Easter eggs pitched at multiple difficulty levels, from straightforward to (in Clarke's words) "how the hell did they figure that out."

No price, exact production quantity, or design-file details were found; the badge was distributed to attendees as part of registration rather than sold. This entry describes the same physical badge as the separately-swept entry `dc19-badge`.
